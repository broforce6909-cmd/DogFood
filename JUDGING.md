## 1. Assignment

Batch assignment: N judges across M submissions with K reviews per submission.
`POST /api/events/{slug}/assignments`, implemented in `app/assignment.py`.

Four constraints, and the distinction between them matters:

| | Constraint | Kind |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Every submission receives exactly K reviews | target |
| 2 | Judge load is balanced to within one submission | target |
| 3 | A judge is never assigned a submission from their own team | **hard** |
| 4 | Track judges only receive submissions in their track | **hard** |

**The hard ones never bend.** If satisfying (1) would require breaking (3), the
algorithm assigns fewer reviews and reports a shortfall naming the submission and
the reason. A platform that quietly assigns a project to someone on its team is
worse than one that says it cannot.

**(1) and (2) can conflict with each other**, and then (1) wins. Six submissions
needing three reviews from four judges is 18 ballots over 4 people, which cannot
be both exact and equal. An unreviewed project is a worse outcome than an
unevenly loaded judge, so the round is covered and `spread` is reported.

### The algorithm

Greedy, most-constrained-first:

1. Compute the eligible judge set for every submission — (3) and (4) applied once,
   in one function.
2. Process submissions in ascending order of eligible-set size. The hardware
   project with two possible reviewers is served before the general-track project
   anyone can take. Doing it the other way round is how a greedy assigner paints
   itself into a corner.
3. For each, take the `K` eligible judges with the lowest current load, shuffling
   first so that equal loads are broken fairly rather than alphabetically.

Load counts ballots the judge **already holds**, so re-running assignment after
inviting one more judge tops the event up instead of double-loading whoever was
assigned first. The `uq_judge_assignments_once` unique index on
`(judge_id, submission_id)` is what makes that true in the database rather than
only in the handler.

`seed` makes a run reproducible: the same seed and the same population give the
same plan, so an organizer can demonstrate that the assignment was not arbitrary.
`dry_run=true` computes and reports a plan without writing it.

**Why greedy rather than min-cost flow.** The constraint graph is tens of judges
against tens of projects, and the objective — balanced load under two hard filters
— is exactly what least-loaded-first greedy optimises. A flow formulation would be
more impressive and would produce the same answer. `tests/test_assignment.py`
asserts the four constraints over eight generated populations rather than one
example, because a conflict-of-interest bug that only appears at 17 judges is
still a conflict-of-interest bug.

### What assignment does *not* do

It does not try to match judges to projects by expertise. Tracks are the only
affinity signal in the model, and inventing a second one from tech tags would be a
heuristic dressed as a feature. Noted as a real gap: a judge who knows Rust is
currently no likelier to review the Rust project.

## 2. The weighted rubric

Criteria and weights are **organizer-configured rows** in `rubric_criteria`, not
constants. The brief's complaint is that the market leader cannot weight criteria
at all, and its closest rival ships one fixed five-criterion rubric shared by
every event on the platform. Here, `POST /api/events/{slug}/criteria` takes a key,
a name, a weight and a score range, and each event has its own.

A criterion's `min_score`/`max_score` are still whole numbers -- they describe the
rubric's own range, e.g. "1-5" -- but a judge's actual score within that range is
continuous, in 0.1 steps (`scores.value` is `Numeric(5, 2)`, not an integer). A 4.3
and a 4.4 are different judgements a judge is allowed to have; forcing them both to
round to "4" would throw away exactly the resolution that most needs to survive
into a close ranking. Nothing below this point cares whether `score(s, j, c)` is a
whole number or not -- the mean, the standard deviation and the z-score are all
defined the same way either way.

For a submission `s` scored by judge `j` across criteria `c`:

```
raw(s, j) = Σ_c  score(s, j, c) × weight(c)  /  Σ_c weight(c)
```

Two consequences worth being explicit about:

**Weights are divided by their own sum, at read time.** An organizer who changes
one weight re-ranks the event from that moment on; they do **not** rescale the
ballots already cast, because nothing stored is ever weighted. Weights of 1 and 2
mean exactly what 10 and 20 mean — asserted in
`test_weights_are_relative_not_absolute`.

**The divisor is the weight of the criteria actually scored**, not of all of them.
A ballot missing a criterion is weighted over what is present rather than scored
as if the gap were a zero; scoring it as zero would punish the project for the
judge's incomplete work. The API refuses to mark such a ballot `complete`, which
is where that rule belongs, and incomplete ballots are excluded from results by
default.

The seeded rubric is deliberately uneven — Impact 3, Execution 3, Craft 2,
Presentation 1 — because an equal rubric would not demonstrate that weighting
happens at all.

## 3. Cross-judge normalization

The problem, restated: a judge who marks everything 3 and a judge who uses the
full 1–5 range are not speaking the same language, and averaging their raw numbers
treats them as if they were. In the fixture data below, one judge's mean is 2.500
and another's is 4.444. Those two are not disagreeing about quality by two points;
they are using different scales.

**Method: per-judge z-score standardisation with shrinkage, rescaled into the
original units.**

```
z(s, j)    = ( raw(s, j) − μ̂_j ) / σ̂_j
norm(s, j) = μ_G + σ_G × z(s, j)

score(s)   = mean over judges j who reviewed s of norm(s, j)
```

where `μ_G` and `σ_G` are the pooled mean and standard deviation of every raw
score in the event, and `μ̂_j`, `σ̂_j` are judge `j`'s own statistics shrunk toward
those global ones.

A judge's harshness cancels; only their *relative ordering* survives, which is the
part that carries information. `test_harshness_cancels_when_orderings_agree`
asserts exactly that: two judges who rank three projects identically but live two
points apart produce identical normalized scores.

The rescaling by `μ_G` and `σ_G` is cosmetic and deliberate. It puts the output
back on the 1–5 scale the rubric is written in, so "4.07" means something to an
organizer. Rankings are identical with or without it.

### Population vs sample standard deviation

`σ` divides by `n`, not `n − 1`. We are describing the spread of the ballots a
judge actually cast — not inferring the variance of a population they were sampled
from — so dividing by `n` is the honest choice. It also means a judge with one
ballot has `σ = 0` rather than undefined, which hands them to the flat-judge rule
below instead of to a `ZeroDivisionError`.

### σ_j → 0: the judge who marks everything a 3

This is the case the brief asks about and the one most likely to be quietly wrong.
Three things you could do, and why two of them are bad:

| Option | Why not |
| --- | --- |
| `σ_j + ε` in the denominator | Turns a flat ballot into enormous z-scores whose sign is decided by floating-point noise. The judge who marked everything 3 would become the most influential voice in the event. |
| Borrow the global `σ_G` | Invents an ordering the judge never expressed. Their scores are all equal, so every project they saw gets the same non-zero offset — which still perturbs comparisons between projects seen by different judge sets. |
| **Map them to z = 0** | What we do. |

A constant ballot means "I am not distinguishing between these". The honest
encoding of that is **z = 0 for every one of their ballots**, which rescales to
exactly `μ_G`: they say "all of these are average", contribute nothing to the
ordering, and cannot drag a project up or down.

Note it is the judge's **own** `σ_j`, before shrinkage, that triggers this.
Shrinkage would otherwise lend them the field's spread and manufacture the
ordering we just said they never expressed.

Tested in `test_a_flat_judge_contributes_exactly_the_global_mean` and
`test_a_flat_judge_does_not_decide_the_ranking` — where the flat judge marks
everything **5**, the most generous ballot possible, and the ranking still comes
entirely from the judge who discriminated.

If *every* judge is flat, `σ_G` is also 0, every z is 0, and everything ties. That
is the correct answer, and `test_every_judge_flat_is_a_dead_heat_not_a_crash`
asserts it rather than discovering it in production.

### Small n: shrinkage

A judge with three assignments has a badly estimated mean, and dividing by their
badly estimated `σ` amplifies the error. Standard treatment, applied to both
statistics:

```
μ̂_j = ( n_j × μ_j + k × μ_G ) / ( n_j + k )
σ̂_j = ( n_j × σ_j + k × σ_G ) / ( n_j + k )

k = 3
```

Read `k` as "the number of imaginary average-judge observations added to every
judge's record". A judge with 3 ballots is weighted half on themselves and half on
the field; a judge with 30 is weighted 91% on themselves.

**Why a constant rather than an estimated prior.** The principled version is a
hierarchical model estimating the between-judge variance and shrinking by its
ratio to within-judge variance. With a dozen judges and a handful of ballots each,
that variance component is itself estimated from almost nothing, and the result is
a more impressive-looking number with no more information in it. `k = 3` is a
stated, inspectable choice; it is published in the API response as `shrinkage_k`
and it is one constant to change. A statistician should read this as "empirical
Bayes with a fixed prior weight" and a deliberately conservative one.

In the table below, Ivan Novak is the case: 3 ballots, own mean 4.444, shrunk to
3.929 — pulled a long way toward the field, because we do not trust three
observations.

### Non-random assignment

Z-scoring assumes each judge saw a comparable slice of quality. If the best
projects were systematically given to one judge, normalizing their scores would
flatten a real difference into a calibration artifact.

This is why **assignment and normalization are one design, not two**. Balanced,
shuffled, conflict-free assignment is what makes "comparable slice" roughly true,
and §1's load balancing is therefore load-bearing for §3's validity, not just
fairness to judges.

**Fixed in Phase 5: normalization is now scoped per track.** Through Phase 4 this
section named the actual weakness -- a judge restricted to one track was still
z-scored against a global mean pooling in tracks they never saw, so a track that
happened to run harsher or draw stronger projects could be mistaken for judge
harshness, and normalization has no way to tell the two apart if it is shown both
tracks' scores mixed together.

`app/scoring.py`'s `normalize_by_track()` partitions ballots by the *submission's*
track -- not the judge's, because a judge with no track restriction reviews across
tracks, and each of their ballots belongs to whichever track that submission is
in -- and runs the same `normalize()` independently within each partition. A judge
is now shrunk toward, and z-scored against, only the judges who scored the same
track: exactly the population they are actually comparable to.

The seeded event demonstrates this directly, and not with the track-restricted
judge you would expect: Priya Rivera, Hana Tanaka and Kwame Osei all review
*both* tracks with no restriction, so each now produces **two** rows in
`GET .../results`' `calibrations` -- one per track -- with genuinely different
statistics in each, visible in §5's proof below (Priya: mean 4.444 in one track,
4.000 in the other). Before this fix, that difference was invisible, silently
averaged into one number that was equally wrong about both tracks.

**What this does not solve, named as plainly as the gap it replaces**: a track's
rescaled output uses that track's own mean/sd for the cosmetic 1-5 rescale, so a
normalized "4.1" in one track and "4.1" in another are not the same claim -- there
is no way to make them the same claim without assuming the tracks are of identical
true quality, which is exactly the assumption normalizing per track exists to stop
making. Ranks stay meaningful across the combined list (each project's rank
reflects what it earned within its own track's comparison basis), but an organizer
who wants numbers directly comparable across tracks wants one prize per track --
already the more common shape for a multi-track hackathon in the first place.

## 4. Role isolation

Not maths, but judging integrity, and the brief weights them together.

**All of it is one function.** `check_access(user, resource, action)` in
[`backend/app/access.py`](backend/app/access.py). The judging rules are three
branches in it, not a pass over the codebase:

- A judge may read a `JudgeAssignment` whose `judges` row has their `user_id`, and
  no other. A peer's ballot is **403** — the status `ARCHITECTURE.md` commits to
  and the acceptance suite is documented as checking.
- A track judge may only touch assignments whose submission is in their track.
  `NULL` track is not a wildcard: an untracked project is nobody's track, so a
  track judge cannot reach it through a data-entry gap.
- A `Score` is governed by the ballot it hangs off. One rule, not two to drift
  apart.

**Organizers may read every ballot and may not write one.** This is the single
place in the codebase where staff privilege stops, and it is deliberate: an
organizer who can author a judge's score can forge a result, and nothing
downstream could tell the forgery from the judgement. They may delete an
assignment — administration — but not author its scores. `PUT .../scores` has no
"on behalf of" parameter at all, which is a stronger guarantee than checking for
one.

Every one of these is asserted twice: as a pure predicate in
`tests/test_access_control.py`, and over HTTP with a bearer token in
`tests/test_judging_isolation.py` — which was written **before** the feature, and
is why the feature has this shape. A unit test tells you the predicate is right;
only the HTTP test tells you it is wired to every route.

**What is still missing:** inter-rater reliability reporting. An organizer can see
*who* scored *what* and *when* — `BALLOT_SCORED` is in the audit log, built in Phase
3 and hash-chained for tamper evidence in Phase 5 — but cannot yet see that two
judges disagreed violently about the same project, which is the signal that should
trigger a third review. Named as the largest remaining judging-integrity gap in
[README.md](README.md#what-it-does-not-do-yet).

### Reading the result

**Every non-flat, non-track-restricted judge calibrates differently per track,
and the proof shows it rather than asserting it.** Priya Rivera, Kwame Osei and
Hana Tanaka all review both tracks with no restriction. Before Phase 5's fix,
each contributed exactly one row above -- their Civic ballots and Logistics
ballots averaged together into a single mean that was quietly wrong about both.
Now each contributes two: Priya is 4.444 in one track and 4.000 in the other,
Kwame 2.667 and 2.333. Those are real, different calibrations, not noise --
mixing them (the old behaviour) would have shrunk each judge toward a field that
included a track they used the scale differently on.

**Foundry and Tidemark swap, and the mechanism is the same one §3 argues for
Ivan Novak specifically.** Foundry (Civic) collects a very generous 5.000 from
Novak, the event's most generous judge on that track. Raw averaging counts that
5.000 at face value and Foundry ranks 4th. Per-track normalization reads it as
"+1.16 standard deviations for a judge whose *Civic* mean is 4.444" -- the
correct comparison, since every other Civic judge's calibration is drawn from
the same Civic-only population -- and Foundry moves to 3rd, ahead of Tidemark,
a Logistics project with no equivalent generous outlier in its own track's
judge pool. Every number in that sentence is in `export/scores.csv` and the
`calibrations` array above.

**Hana Tanaka still changes nothing, in both tracks now instead of one.** Three
ballots per track, every one a 3.000, `sd = 0.000` in each. Normalized, all six
still map to the global mean (3.413, the rescale point, computed across the
whole event) and contribute nothing to either track's ordering -- the fix did
not touch this property, because a flat judge's own `sd` decides it before any
population is chosen.

### What this proof does *not* show

Honesty about the limit of the argument above, unchanged from Phase 4 except
for the one line it closes: normalization still corrects for **judge
generosity**, not for **unequal review counts** -- Pocketful and Foundry each
have four reviews, Switchyard, Tidemark and Crosshatch have three, and a mean
over four differently-calibrated judges is not perfectly comparable to a mean
over three even after standardising each.

~~The fix is per-track normalization~~ -- **done in Phase 5**, argued above and
in §3. What per-track normalization does *not* claim, stated as plainly as the
gap it replaces: a normalized "4.1" in Civic and a normalized "4.1" in
Logistics are not the same claim, because each is rescaled using its own
track's mean and spread. Ranks stay meaningful within the combined list; raw
score comparison across tracks does not, and never did.

### Honest caveats on this proof

- **Six projects and four judges is a small event.** The direction of the effect is
  real; the magnitude on 40 projects would differ.
- **The fixture is constructed**, not observed. The judges are harsh/generous/flat
  by design, which is the point — but it means this demonstrates that the method
  does what it claims, not that real judges look like this. (Hackathon Raptors have
  35 events of real data; we would like to run this against them.)
- **The fixture is not built by the assignment algorithm.** Every judge reviews
  every project they are eligible for, deliberately, so that calibration
  differences are the only thing moving the ranking. With algorithmic assignment,
  review-set differences would be a second confound and the example would teach
  less.
- **Small per-track samples mean heavier shrinkage.** Splitting by track halves
  the ballots each cross-track judge contributes to their own calibration (6 to
  3 each), which is honestly reflected in the shrunk statistics above pulling
  harder toward the field than the pre-Phase-5 numbers did -- the correct
  response to knowing less about a judge in any one track, not an artifact to
  explain away.

## 6. Pairwise judging

§3 is a *correction*: judges keep using a 1-5 scale, and the maths accounts for
the fact that they use it differently. Pairwise judging is an *avoidance*: stop
asking for a number at all. Show a judge two projects and ask which is better --
a question almost everyone answers consistently even when they would place wildly
different numbers on a scale -- and recover a ranking from the comparisons with
the [Bradley-Terry model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bradley%E2%80%93Terry_model),
the same approach [Gavel](https://gavel.mit.edu/) uses for exactly this reason.
Named as "the more interesting answer" through Phase 4; built in Phase 5.

**Additive, not a replacement.** `Event.pairwise_enabled` is its own toggle, and
an event may run both this and the scored rubric -- an organizer is not asked to
choose once and commit. Both share the judging window
(`judging_opens_at`/`judging_closes_at`): they are two different mechanisms for
the same phase of an event, not two separate phases.

### The model, in one paragraph

Every submission has a latent strength; the probability it beats another in one
comparison is its strength divided by the sum of both. Fitted from every
comparison across every judge by the MM (minorization-maximization) algorithm
([Hunter](https://sites.stat.washington.edu/fewster/stat531/hunter2004.pdf)),
which converges to the same answer maximum-likelihood estimation would, without
needing a closed form that does not exist for this model once there are more than
two items. The full account, including the two things a naive implementation
gets wrong, is the module docstring in
[`backend/app/pairwise.py`](backend/app/pairwise.py):

* **Ties** count as half a win for each side rather than being discarded --
  thrown away, they would tell the model less than it actually knows (that the
  two are close).
* **Disconnected components and cold start** -- a submission nobody has
  compared yet, or two clusters of projects that were never compared against
  each other -- are handled by regularizing every submission's rating toward a
  fixed, phantom "average project" anchor. Without it, Bradley-Terry's
  likelihood has no finite answer for an uncompared item; with it, an
  uncompared submission still appears in the ranking, sitting exactly at the
  anchor rather than vanishing or crashing the fit.

**A judge who never compares two specific projects is not a gap to route around.**
Bradley-Terry only needs the *aggregate* win/loss graph across every judge to be
connected enough to compare everyone transitively -- it does not need any one
judge, or even any one pair, to be seen more than once. The proof below is proof
of exactly this: two judges, four comparisons each, and every one of six projects
ends up on a single ranking even though most pairs were never directly compared
by anybody.

### How a pair is chosen

`app/pairwise.pick_next_pair()` prefers, in order: the two *eligible* projects
with the fewest total comparisons so far (spreading coverage rather than letting
an early pair dominate the count forever), among those, the first pair *this*
judge has not already answered (a repeat teaches the model nothing a fresh pair
would not teach better). If a judge has exhausted every pair in a small event, it
falls back to serving the least-compared pair again rather than refusing outright
-- a repeat is weaker signal, not wrong signal, and Bradley-Terry's fit does not
break the way a rubric would if asked to score the same ballot twice.

Eligibility itself mirrors `app/assignment.py`'s rules for scored assignment
exactly: only `submitted` projects, a track judge sees only their own track, and
a judge is never shown their own team's project.

### Coverage: is this round actually balanced

`pick_next_pair`'s greedy heuristic is not a guarantee -- nothing stops one
project from ending up with more comparisons than another if judging stops
early or unevenly across the field. Rather than make an organizer infer that
from scanning the per-project comparison-count column, `GET
.../pairwise/results` exposes `coverage` directly: total projects, total
comparisons, min/max/mean comparisons per project, and the percentage of
projects compared at least once (`app/pairwise.coverage()`, unit-tested in
`tests/test_pairwise.py`). Self-comparison and duplicate-pair prevention are
handled upstream of this stat, not by it: `PairwiseVoteIn`'s
`_distinct_and_consistent` validator plus the `ck_pairwise_distinct_submissions`
database constraint make a self-comparison a 422/constraint violation before a
row is ever written, and the pairwise data model has no notion of a "duplicate"
comparison to guard against -- two judges (or the same judge, per the fallback
above) comparing the same pair is expected and simply adds another data point
to the same fit, not an error.

What this run demonstrates, concretely:

* **Crosshatch never played Quietline, Switchyard or Tidemark directly**, and
  still ranks clearly above all three -- inferred transitively through the
  comparisons it and its opponents *did* have, exactly the property named
  above.
* **Two ties landed as fractional records** (Crosshatch 2.5-0.5, Pocketful
  2.5-0.5), not as discarded data or a crash.
* **Three of five judges never touched this event's pairwise queue at all**
  (0 comparisons each) -- the ranking is still complete and every project still
  has a place, because eligibility and the fit do not require full participation.
* **Every project got at least 2 comparisons and none got more than 3** (see
  `COVERAGE` above) -- `pick_next_pair`'s "least-compared eligible project
  first" rule did what it is documented to do, at this scale, without a
  round-robin scheduler forcing it.
* **Rankings 2 through 5 are close** (0.681 down to -0.370) on a small number of
  comparisons, which is the honest picture Bradley-Terry gives with this little
  data -- not a flaw in the run, the same way a handful of ballots would not
  produce a confident normalized ranking either.

### Honest caveats

- **Eight comparisons on six projects is a small sample**, deliberately, so this
  proof stays fast to reproduce. A real event would accumulate far more before
  anyone reads the ranking as final.
- **No Davidson tie parameter.** A tie is treated as exactly half a win each,
  which is simpler than modelling "close" as its own quantity separately from
  "no information", and this project's use case does not need that
  distinction. Named as a simplification, not discovered as a bug.
- **Coverage is a heuristic, not a design that guarantees balance.**
  `pick_next_pair` greedily prefers the least-compared eligible projects; it does
  not guarantee every project ends up with the same number of comparisons the
  way round-robin scheduling would, and it does not need to for the fit to
  produce a ranking -- just to produce a *good* one, which is a claim about
  degree, not correctness.

## 7. Disagreement, third review, and consistency flags

Normalization corrects for how a judge uses the scale. It does not, and cannot,
correct for two judges who genuinely disagree about which project is better --
that disagreement is exactly the signal an organizer needs to see, not
statistical noise to smooth over.

**Disagreement.** `GET /{slug}/results/disagreement` runs `app/scoring.py`'s
`disagreement()` over the same `Results` `/results` computes, and flags any
submission whose judges' *normalized* scores differ, on average pairwise, by
more than `DISAGREEMENT_THRESHOLD` (1.5) points. Normalized, not raw --
two judges giving raw 2 and 4 might just be one harsh and one generous judge
agreeing perfectly, which normalization already accounts for; disagreement
*after* that correction is about the project. `judge_deviation()` runs
alongside it, ranking judges by how far their normalized scores sit from the
consensus on projects they shared with other reviewers -- a different
question from calibration, and one calibration cannot answer on its own.

**Third review.** `POST /{slug}/results/disagreement/{submission_id}/third-review`
routes a flagged submission to one more, previously uninvolved judge, chosen by
the exact same `plan_assignments` eligibility rules batch assignment uses (own
team excluded, track scope respected, no duplicate reviewer, no recused judge --
see below) asked for one more reviewer on one submission instead of a whole
event. This is purely additive: the ballots that disagreed are never touched,
changed, or read-modified by this call, only marked `is_adjudication=true` on
the new one so an organizer can tell an ordinary review from a called-in
adjudication at a glance. There is no auto-overwrite here to name as a rule,
because there is nothing in this path that writes over an existing score --
`normalize()` simply averages the third ballot in with the rest once it is
complete, the same as any other.

Live against the seeded fixture (`raptors-winter`):

```
GET /results/disagreement -> threshold 1.5, needs_review_count 1
  Foundry: 4 reviews, sd 1.13, mean abs pairwise diff 1.61 -- needs_review: true
  (five other projects: mean abs pairwise diff 0.79-1.40, none crossed the threshold)

POST /results/disagreement/<Foundry's id>/third-review -> 409
  "No eligible judge left for a third review -- every active judge is already
  assigned to this submission, on the submitting team, recused, or outside
  this project's track."
```

That 409 is not a bug -- it is the honest answer for this fixture. All five of
the event's judges are already on Foundry's ballot (four complete, one
pending), so there genuinely is nobody left to call in; a real event with a
larger judge pool would return a `ThirdReviewResult` naming the reviewer
instead. Both outcomes are exercised in `tests/test_judging_api.py`.

**Consistency flags.** `GET /{slug}/results/consistency` surfaces two signals,
both for organizer review only and never worded as an accusation (see
`app/scoring.py`'s `ConsistencyFlag`): `near_identical_score_flags()` catches a
judge whose raw scores barely move across `CONSISTENCY_MIN_REVIEWS` (4) or more
ballots -- not `flat` (which fires correctly on a single ballot's own zero
spread, because normalization needs to know that judge contributes no
ordering *at all*), but a judge with real evidence behind them who used the
scale as if it barely existed. `fast_completion_flags()` catches a judge who
finished one ballot and then another within `FAST_COMPLETION_SECONDS` (30) --
an honest proxy, not a precise measurement, since `completed_at` is the only
timestamp this system records and it says nothing about when a judge actually
opened the ballot. On the seeded fixture above, this endpoint returns an empty
`flags` list: five judges, ordinary review pacing, nothing suspicious to
surface -- the same endpoint that would flag a real problem correctly reports
"nothing to see" when there genuinely is nothing to see.

**Conflict of interest.** Team membership already keeps a judge off their own
team's project, structurally, in both scored assignment (`app/assignment.py`'s
`_eligible`) and pairwise (`_eligible_submission_ids`) -- no organizer action
needed. `models.JudgeRecusal` adds the conflict structure cannot see: a judge
who personally knows a team, or otherwise should not score one specific
project. Either the judge themself (`POST
/api/judging/assignments/{id}/recuse`, refused once the ballot is already
`COMPLETE` -- an organizer's call at that point, not a unilateral one) or an
organizer (`POST /{slug}/judges/{judge_id}/recusals`) can record one; from
then on, both `plan_assignments` and pairwise eligibility exclude that pairing
exactly like team membership, and any *incomplete* assignment already linking
the two is removed. A completed ballot is a historical record and survives a
later recusal untouched.

**Explicitly not built**: a unified "judging integrity" dashboard combining
all of the above into one view. It is lower-value than the signals above
relative to its build cost, and is named here as a deliberate scoping decision,
not an oversight. (This paragraph used to list a judge calibration exercise --
a practice round against pre-scored reference projects -- as not built as well.
It was built later, deliberately small; see §10.)

## 8. The winner/community-vote split

`events.winner_slots` and `events.community_vote_slots` (Phase 6, Part 3) turn
`normalized_rank` -- already computed by §3's method, for free -- into a label:
the top `winner_slots` ranks are outright winners, the next
`community_vote_slots` are the *community tier*, and everyone else is
unlabelled. `app/routers/results.py`'s `compute_tiers()` is the whole of it:
a dict lookup from submission id to `"winner"` / `"community_tier"`,
recomputed on every read rather than stored, the same as the ranking it reads
from.

The community tier then gets its own voting round: `app/routers/voting.py`
scopes the whole community-vote mechanism -- the ballot, the tally, vote
validation -- to exactly that subset once `community_vote_slots > 0`, reusing
the identical `Voter`/`Vote` tables and endpoints every other event's voting
already uses. Nothing about *how* a vote is counted changes; only *which
projects* are on the ballot. Eligibility to vote in that round is separate
again: registered for the event at least 7 days before it started
(`Event.community_vote_eligible`, `app/models.py`), which overrides the
event's general `voting_access` rather than adding to it -- see DATA-MODEL.md's
Phase 6 section for the full mechanism and why.

**Ties at the boundary are not specially handled**, which follows directly
from §8's "ties are not broken" below: if two submissions tie for the last
winner slot, competition ranking (1, 1, 3) means both carry `normalized_rank
== winner_slots`, so `compute_tiers()` labels both `winner` -- the cutoff can
admit more than `winner_slots` submissions on a tie, never fewer. The same is
true at the community-tier boundary. This is the ranking scheme's existing
behavior, not a new rule for the split to work around.

- ~~No winner/community-vote split~~ -- **done in Phase 6.** See §8.
- **No expertise matching** in assignment. See §1.
- **Ties are not broken.** Equal normalized scores share a rank (competition

## 10. Judge calibration (optional, deliberately small)

`normalize()` (§3) corrects for a harsh or generous judge *after* scoring. This
is the earlier, coarser question: which judges should an organizer talk to
*before* scoring starts?

An organizer adds one or two **practice projects** with the score they expect for
every criterion. Each judge scores them like a real ballot. For each criterion the
deviation is `(judge - expected) / (max - min)` -- a fraction of that criterion's
own range, so 1-5 and 0-10 rubrics count equally. A judge's **mean signed
deviation** at or below **-0.10** is flagged `harsh`, at or above **+0.10**
`generous`, otherwise `aligned` (0.10 of a 1-5 rubric is 0.4 points). The **mean
absolute deviation** is shown next to it, because a judge who is far too high on
one project and far too low on another averages to zero and is still not
calibrated. No flag is issued until every practice project is fully scored.

What it does **not** do: it changes no score, feeds nothing into `gather()` or
the results, and never shows a judge the expected values. With one or two practice
projects it is a noisy estimate and 0.10 is a starting point, not a finding -- a
prompt for a conversation, not a verdict. Code: `app/calibration.py` (pure maths),
`app/routers/calibration.py`; tests: `tests/test_calibration.py`, and
`e2e/tests/calibration.spec.ts` for the whole flow through the UI.
